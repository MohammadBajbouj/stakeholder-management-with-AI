# Presentation and Storytelling for Stakeholder Alignment

Stakeholder presentations are not just information transfer. They are moments to
create shared understanding, ask for decisions, resolve ambiguity, and build
confidence. A strong stakeholder presentation is short, structured,
evidence-led, and clear about what should happen next.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

- Choose the right presentation structure for a stakeholder situation.
- Use storytelling to make complex product and AI topics understandable.
- Prepare decision briefings with clear asks and trade-offs.
- Use visuals without overloading the audience.
- Give and receive feedback on stakeholder presentations.

## Know the Audience

Before creating slides, answer:

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Who is in the room? | Different stakeholders need different evidence. |
| What decision or alignment is needed? | The presentation should have a purpose. |
| What do they already believe? | You may need to confirm, correct, or reframe assumptions. |
| What are they worried about? | Concerns should be addressed directly. |
| What should they do afterwards? | The close should make action obvious. |

## Useful Structures

| Structure | Best for |
| --- | --- |
| Problem, solution, benefit | Persuading stakeholders to support a direction. |
| Situation, complication, recommendation | Executive decision briefings. |
| Past, present, future | Project updates and retrospectives. |
| Options and trade-offs | Decisions with competing stakeholder needs. |
| Three-act story | Explaining a journey from challenge to outcome. |

## Storytelling for Product and Project Managers

Stories help stakeholders understand why something matters. They connect data,
behavior, constraints, and decisions.

For product and AI work, stories often follow this pattern:

1. A user or team has a painful current workflow.
2. The current process creates risk, delay, cost, or frustration.
3. The proposed product changes the workflow.
4. The team shows evidence from research, prototype, or metrics.
5. Stakeholders are asked to make a decision or support the next step.

## The Hero's Journey, Used Carefully

The hero's journey is a narrative model associated with Joseph Campbell. It can
help structure a stakeholder story, but the stakeholder should not be forced
into a dramatic myth. Use it lightly: describe a current challenge, the tools or
support needed, and the better future after change.

![Hero's journey diagram](assets/wikimedia-heroes-journey.png)

_Source: Wikimedia Commons, "Heroesjourney.svg", public domain._

In stakeholder communication, the audience is often the hero. The product or
project team is the guide that helps them reach a better outcome.

## Decision Briefing Template

Use this structure when you need a decision:

```text
Context:
What changed or why this decision matters now.

Decision needed:
The exact decision or approval requested.

Recommendation:
The option the team recommends.

Evidence:
User data, technical analysis, risk assessment, cost, or compliance input.

Trade-offs:
What we gain, what we give up, and what remains uncertain.

Next step:
Who does what by when.
```

## Visual Design Principles

Good visuals reduce cognitive load. They should make the message easier to
understand, not compete with the speaker.

| Principle | Practical behavior |
| --- | --- |
| One message per slide | Make the title the takeaway. |
| Prefer visuals for relationships | Use diagrams for flows, ownership, and trade-offs. |
| Use tables sparingly | Use tables for comparison, not dense reading. |
| Show the decision | Make the recommendation and ask visible. |
| Remove decorative clutter | Keep attention on evidence and action. |

## Accessible Presentations

W3C guidance emphasizes that audiences may not be able to see, hear, or access a
shared screen in the same way. Accessible delivery makes the presentation
stronger for remote, multilingual, and time-constrained stakeholders too.

- Give every slide a unique, meaningful title.
- Use sufficient contrast and readable type.
- Add alternative text to informative images.
- Explain relevant visual information aloud.
- Do not use color as the only way to distinguish categories.
- Provide captions for video and audio.
- Share materials in a format participants can access directly.
- Keep a written record of decisions and actions outside the slide deck.

## Handling Questions and Challenge

Questions are evidence about stakeholder needs. Listen fully, clarify the
concern, and answer at the appropriate level.

Use this pattern:

1. Acknowledge the question.
2. Clarify what the stakeholder needs to decide or understand.
3. Answer briefly with evidence.
4. State uncertainty honestly.
5. Record any follow-up, owner, and deadline.

If you do not know, say so. A precise follow-up builds more trust than an
improvised answer.

## Practice: Three-Minute Stakeholder Briefing

Prepare a three-minute briefing for one stakeholder group:

1. Name the audience.
2. State the decision or alignment you need.
3. Use one simple story or scenario.
4. Show one piece of evidence.
5. End with a clear ask.

Record yourself if possible, then review:

- Was the ask clear?
- Did the story match the audience's concerns?
- Did the evidence support the recommendation?
- Was the conclusion memorable?

## Check Your Understanding

### Question 1

Why should a stakeholder presentation start with the audience and decision
need?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

Because the same project information should be framed differently for different
audiences. A sponsor, legal reviewer, pilot user, and engineering lead need
different evidence and different asks.

</details>

### Question 2

What is the risk of putting too much text on slides?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

The audience reads instead of listening, important messages become harder to
find, and the speaker loses control of the narrative.

</details>

### Question 3

How can storytelling help with technical AI topics?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

Storytelling connects technical detail to a human or business situation. It can
explain why model behavior, data quality, or governance matters in a concrete
workflow.

</details>

### Question 4

What should a decision briefing always include?

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

It should include the decision needed, recommendation, evidence, trade-offs, and
next step. Without these, stakeholders may understand the topic but still not
know what to do.

</details>

## Key Takeaways

- Stakeholder presentations should create alignment or decisions, not just
  share information.
- Structure should match the audience and purpose.
- Stories help people understand why a technical decision matters.
- Visuals should simplify, compare, or focus attention.
- End with a specific ask and owner.
